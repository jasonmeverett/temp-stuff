How the TP920 was decoded

Finding the stream

The Mac’s Bluetooth LE scan found TP920 advertising service 1086fff0-3343-4817-8bb2-b32206336ce8. Connecting revealed the FFF1 command characteristic and the FFF2 notification characteristic.

I subscribed to FFF2 and initially received zero measurements. I then sent the startup command 01 09 8a 7a 13 b7 3e d6 8b 67 c2 a0 to FFF1 and received 0x30 measurement packets. The command came from a community capture for the same ThermoPro service. Community capture

The startup packet begins with the command type 01 and payload length 09. The final a0 is the low byte of the sum of all preceding bytes. The meaning of the nine payload bytes is still unknown.

Reading the bytes

This example packet uses a synthetic temperature. The first byte, 30, identifies a temperature report. The next byte, 0f, says the payload is 15 bytes long. I verified the checksum and probe offsets on live TP920 notifications. Byte numbering starts at zero. ThermoPro packet analysis

30 0f 64 0f 00 ff ff 02 35 ff ff ff ff ff ff ff ff df
Probe 1 · bytes 5–6
ff ff means the probe returned no reading.
Probe 2 · bytes 7–8
Each hex digit represents a decimal digit. 02 35 is 235 tenths of a degree, or 23.5°C (74.3°F).
Checksum · byte 17
The low byte of the sum of the preceding bytes is df.

From packet to chart

Every 10 seconds, the recorder saves the freshest validated packet to SQLite. The dashboard receives the committed row and plots both probes. Missing or unrecognized values appear as gaps. The chart shows the last 24 hours, and SQLite retains the full log.
